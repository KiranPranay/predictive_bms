The project in two sentences

This project protects a battery pack of four lithium-ion cells. An ESP32 controller measures every cell five times a second, uses a heat model to find heat that should not be there, and disconnects the pack early, long before the cells reach a fixed temperature limit.

The hardware is not built yet. Everything you show is a simulation: on the computer, with the real circuit values and the real controller program. Always say "in the simulation" when you give a result.

3. Part 1: the demonstration dashboard

The dashboard shows the whole system working on a simulated pack: four cells, their heat, the hardware BMS, the sensors, and the same controller program that will run on the ESP32. A second copy of the pack runs beside it, protected only by fixed limits, like a normal BMS. So every fault shows how much earlier this project acts.

Start it

  1. Double-click 1 Demo - double-click.bat. A black window opens. It says that the demo is running. Keep this black window open for the whole presentation. Closing it stops the demo.
  2. Your browser opens the dashboard by itself. If it does not, open Edge or Chrome and type http://127.0.0.1:8080/ in the address bar.
  3. Wait a few seconds until the numbers on the page start to change.

What is on the page

  • Guided demonstrations: nine buttons. Each button starts one story on a fresh pack. A text box tells what is happening, step by step. It has Pause, Restart and Stop buttons.
  • Predictive layer against a fixed-limit BMS: the comparison of the two packs.
  • Cell cards: for each cell, its voltage, temperature, estimated core temperature, abnormal heat (in watts) and its risk level.
  • System: a live drawing of the pack, the relays and the sensors.
  • Charts: cell temperatures, abnormal heat, and each 5-minute forecast drawn against the real reading.
  • Verification: all 30 test scenarios with their checks, at the bottom.

Show the demonstrations in this order

Click one button, let it run to the end, read the result box, then click the next. Times are approximate and change by a few seconds between runs.

4. Part 2: the circuits in KiCad

KiCad is the circuit design program used in industry. Here it holds four small circuits, each with its own simulation. The part values are the ones the prototype uses. Each circuit is one folder in 2 KiCad circuits.

How to open and run any of the four

  1. In the folder, open one circuit folder, for example thermal-model. Double-click the file ending in .kicad_pro. KiCad opens.
  2. In the KiCad window, double-click the file ending in .kicad_sch (or click Schematic Editor). The circuit appears.
  3. In the Schematic Editor menu, click Inspect → Simulator. The simulator window opens.
  4. Click Run (the green triangle ▶). If a window asks for the analysis, it is already filled in from the blue command written on the sheet (for example .tran 1 3610): click OK.
  5. Click Add Signals, tick the signals named below, and click OK. The graph appears.
Say: This is the real circuit with the real part values, drawn in KiCad. KiCad's built-in simulator, ngspice, calculates it. KiCad's electrical rules check finds no errors.

Circuit 1: cell-taps (board A)

  • Run: this sheet uses .op, the "operating point": the steady voltages.
  • Read: the voltages of ADC1, ADC2, ADC3, ADC4 in the results list: about 0.59, 1.19, 1.78, 2.38 V.
  • Point at: all four are below 3.0 V, the safe limit for the ADS1115 input (the Gate 1 stop rule).
Say: Each cell tap goes through a 330 kΩ and 56 kΩ divider, so 16.8 V becomes about 2.4 V, which the ADC can read safely. The 10 kΩ and 100 nF filter removes noise.

Circuit 2: ntc-divider (board B)

  • Run: this sheet sweeps the temperature of the NTC from −20 to 100 °C (.dc TEMP -20 100 1).
  • Add signal: V(NTC_ADC). The graph shows the ADC voltage falling as the temperature rises.
  • Point at: 1.65 V at 25 °C, about 1.00 V at 45 °C (the charge limit) and about 0.76 V at 55 °C (the discharge limit).
Say: An NTC's resistance falls as it gets hotter. With a 10 kΩ resistor above it, the voltage at the ESP32 tells the temperature. It stays in the ESP32's accurate range from about 3 °C to above 100 °C.

Circuit 3: current-sense

  • Run: this sheet sweeps the pack current from −5 A (charging) to +5 A (discharging) (.dc I1 -5 5 0.1).
  • Add signal: the voltage across the shunt, V(/SENSE_P) − V(/SENSE_N). In the simulator, use the user-defined signal box (in the signals menu) and type V(/SENSE_P)-V(/SENSE_N). If you cannot find it, add V(/SENSE_P) and V(/SENSE_N) and explain the small gap between them.
  • Point at: 25.0 mV at 5 A. The INA226 can read up to about 81.9 mV, so there is a large margin. Discharge reads positive.
Say: A 5 milliohm resistor in the pack's path makes a small voltage proportional to the current. The INA226 chip reads it in both directions, charge and discharge.

Circuit 4: thermal-model

  • Run: this sheet simulates one hour after 1 W of heat starts in the cell's core (.tran 1 3610).
  • Add signals: V(/T_CORE) and V(/T_SURF). Here 1 volt means 1 °C.
  • Point at: both start at 25 °C. After one hour the core is at about 54.5 °C and still rising slowly. It would settle at 57 °C.
Say: Heat flows like current. A heat capacity behaves like a capacitor, and a thermal resistance like a resistor. So the cell's heat model is a simple RC circuit. 1 volt stands for 1 °C and 1 ampere for 1 watt. The ESP32 runs this same model five times a second.
Tip. On the projector, zoom the graph with the mouse wheel, or use the View menu to zoom.

5. Part 3: the model check in GNU Octave

GNU Octave runs MATLAB-style programs (files ending in .m). We use it to check the heat model and the estimator a second, independent way. The same files also run in MATLAB.

Open Octave in the right folder

  1. Start GNU Octave (GUI) from the Start menu.
  2. Find the File Browser panel (on the left). Use its folder box at the top, or the Current Directory box above the Command Window, to go to the folder 3 Octave model check. The panel now lists thermal_step.m, crosscheck.m and the other files.

Run 1: thermal_step (a few seconds)

  1. Double-click thermal_step.m. It opens in the Editor.
  2. Press F5 (or click the Run button ▶ in the Editor). Another way: click in the Command Window, type thermal_step and press Enter.
  3. A figure opens: the core (orange) and surface (blue) temperatures over one hour after 1 W of heat. The Command Window prints the temperatures at 1, 5, 20 and 60 minutes, and the two time constants, 8.4 s and 1422 s.
Say: This is the same heat model as in KiCad, solved by a different method. KiCad and Octave agree to within 0.0002 °C.

Run 2: crosscheck (one scenario in about a minute; all four take a few minutes)

  1. In the Command Window type crosscheck({{'load_defect'}}) and press Enter: one scenario. (crosscheck alone runs all four; run that the day before.)
  2. Octave starts bms_sim.exe, the simulator, which runs the real controller program and writes a log. Then Octave's own copy of the estimator reads the same log and calculates the same numbers.
  3. A figure opens with four panels. Top: the core temperature and the abnormal heat of each cell; the solid (Octave) and dashed (controller) lines lie on top of each other. Bottom: the difference between them: only a few thousandths of a degree and of a watt. (The same figure is in the Build Guide, section 16.5.)
Say: The controller's estimator was written again in Octave and given the same sensor data. Both give the same core temperature and abnormal heat, to a few thousandths. This shows the controller program does what the model says.

6. Part 4: the block diagrams in Scilab Xcos

Xcos is the block-diagram simulator inside Scilab, the free tool like Simulink. Each model is a drawing of blocks joined by arrows. A scope block draws the result.

How to run a model

  1. Open the folder 4 Scilab Xcos models and double-click the model file (.zcos). Scilab starts and Xcos opens the diagram. (If it does not: start Scilab, click Applications → Xcos, then File → Open and choose the file.)
  2. Click Start, the black triangle ▶ in the Xcos toolbar.
  3. Wait until the simulation ends. The scope windows open with the graphs.

Model 1: cell_thermal_model.zcos

The heat model of one cell as blocks: 1 W of heat starts in the core at 10 s, the air is at 25 °C, and the simulation runs for one hour. The scope shows the core and the surface temperatures. It is the same model as the KiCad thermal-model sheet and Octave's thermal_step.

Point at: [[XCOS-A]]

Say: Here the same heat model is built from blocks, as engineers do in Simulink. The heat goes in on the left, the model is in the middle, and the scope shows the temperatures.

Model 2: abnormal_heat_detection.zcos

One cell carries 3 A. Its normal Joule heat (I²R) is known. At 10 minutes, 1 W of extra, unknown heat starts: the "abnormal" heat. An observer block, a fixed-gain version of the controller's Kalman filter, watches only the surface temperature and estimates the abnormal heat. Two trips are compared:

  • Predictive trip: when the estimated abnormal heat reaches 0.75 W (about 1 °C per minute, the architecture's trip level);
  • Fixed-limit trip: when the surface temperature reaches 55 °C.

The scopes show the temperatures, the estimated against the true abnormal heat, and the two trip signals.

Point at: [[XCOS-B]]

Say: The observer sees only the surface temperature, like the real NTC sensor. It works out how much heat is not explained by the current. The predictive trip comes long before the fixed 55 °C limit.

9. The day before: checklist

  • ☐ KiCad 10, GNU Octave 10.3.0 and Scilab 2026.0.1 are installed. Each one has been opened once (KiCad's library question is answered).
  • ☐ The zip is unblocked and extracted to a short folder, for example C:\PredictiveBMS (not inside OneDrive).
  • ☐ The demo starts, and all five demonstrations of section 3 run to the end.
  • ☐ Each of the four KiCad circuits runs, and you know which signals to add.
  • ☐ In Octave, thermal_step and the full crosscheck have run once.
  • ☐ Both Xcos models run and show their scopes.
  • ☐ You have read the Viva Notes, and practised the 15-minute plan two times with a timer.
  • ☐ Laptop charged; charger and HDMI adapter in the bag; the display set to Duplicate (Windows key + P).
  • ☐ Sleep turned off (Settings → System → Power) and notifications off (Focus assist / Do not disturb).
  • ☐ A copy of the whole PredictiveBMS folder on a pen drive.

Nothing in the project needs the internet during the presentation.
